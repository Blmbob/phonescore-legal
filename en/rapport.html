Understanding the report

A report is useless if you don't know what to do with it. Here's what each line means, and above all what it should make you check before handing over the money.

The verdict

At the top of the report, a single color sums it all up. It doesn't replace reading the lines, but it tells you right away whether the deal can move forward.

Safe device

No block detected. Still inspect the device physically: a report covers its identity and status, not its actual condition.

Caution

One or more points need your attention: missing information, refurbished device, carrier lock. Negotiable, but go in informed.

Don't buy

A block makes the device unusable or illegal to resell. No discount makes up for that: the problem cannot be fixed.

Security

The two lines that make or break a purchase.

iCloud lock (Find My)

"Find My iPhone" is active: the device stays tied to its owner's Apple account. After a factory reset, it will ask for their Apple ID and password again.

No one can unlock it — not a repair shop, not Apple without the original proof of purchase, not PhoneScore. An iCloud-locked iPhone is only worth its spare parts.

Do this: ask the seller to turn off "Find My" in front of you, in Settings → their name → Find My. Then run the check again. If they refuse or stall, walk away from the deal.

Blacklist (GSMA)

The IMEI has been reported — stolen, lost, or unpaid with a carrier. The report is shared between carriers in many countries.

A reported device can be cut off the network at any time. It will still work on Wi-Fi, which lets a seller show it "working fine" without quite lying.

Do this: don't buy it. Beyond the risk of it being cut off, knowingly reselling a device reported stolen exposes you personally.

Device

Model, capacity, color

What the manufacturer actually built under this IMEI, whatever the device's screen or the seller's listing says.

Do this: compare with the listing. An iPhone 13 sold as a 14, a 128 GB claimed as 256, a repainted case: the gap shows up here and nowhere else.

IMEI, IMEI2, serial number, MEID

The device's identifiers. IMEI2 only appears on models that handle two lines.

Do this: dial *#06# on the device and compare it with the report. If they differ, the seller gave you the IMEI of a different phone.

Network

SIM-lock (carrier lock)

A locked device only accepts SIM cards from its original carrier — often a foreign one, so unusable here.

Unlocking is sometimes possible through that carrier, but it assumes the contract is paid off, and it is never guaranteed.

Do this: insert your own SIM card before paying. It is the one test that settles it in thirty seconds.

SIM cards and eSIM

Shows whether the device handles two lines. Importantly, the report flags models sold in the United States from the iPhone 14 onward: they have no physical SIM tray at all and work with eSIM only.

Do this: if this alert appears, check that your carrier supports eSIM before buying. Otherwise, the device will never get signal.

Country of purchase

The market the device was built for. It determines the carrier lock, the SIM configuration, and sometimes hardware details — some Japanese models, for instance, cannot turn off the camera shutter sound.

Warranty

Purchase date, repair coverage expiry, AppleCare

Remaining Apple coverage, when the information is available. A purchase date much older than what the seller claims is a signal in itself.

Do this: use it to negotiate. A device out of warranty isn't worth the price of a covered one.

Device history

Refurbished, replaced, loaner, or demo unit

A refurbished device has been restored by Apple: that is not a defect, but it should not sell at new-device price.

Replaced means this unit was provided in exchange for a defective one. A demo unit came from a store and was not meant for resale.

Do this: none of these statuses rule out buying. They justify a lower price, and a seller who didn't mention them tells you something about themselves.

What a report doesn't tell you. It covers the device's identity and administrative status, not its physical condition. A worn battery, a screen replaced with an unofficial part, water damage: none of that shows up. The information comes from third-party databases that can be incomplete or out of date. A clean report is a green light, not a guarantee — always inspect the device.
